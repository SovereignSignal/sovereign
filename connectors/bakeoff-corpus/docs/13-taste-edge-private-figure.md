# The two-visit scoping pass

Writing this down properly because I have now run it four times and it works, and
because I keep rebuilding it from memory at the start of every engagement.

The problem it solves: a utility asks what it would cost to instrument a ring,
and the honest answer depends on things nobody can see from a drawing. Chamber
access, cable routes, whether the isolation valves turn, and whether the asset
register matches the ground. Quote from the drawing and you are wrong in one
direction; survey everything first and you have done half the job for free.

The two-visit scoping pass splits the difference.

**Visit one, dry.** Half a day, no water touched, no lids lifted. Walk the ring
with the asset register in hand and check three things only: does the chamber
exist where the register says, can a vehicle get within hose reach, and is there
a power or comms path. Score every point green, amber, or red on those three
alone. Resist the urge to look inside. The output is a marked-up register, and it
is usually the first time the utility has seen how wrong its own register is.

**Between visits.** Sample the ambers. Pick a fifth of them, weighted toward the
ones that are amber for different reasons, and price those properly. The sample
is what turns the amber population into a number with a stated error bar rather
than a contingency percentage pulled out of the air.

**Visit two, wet.** One day, lids off, on the sampled points only. This is the
visit that finds the standing water, the silted stems, the seized valves. The
findings are extrapolated across the amber population using the sample, not
across the whole ring, because greens are cheap and reds are being excluded
anyway.

What comes out is a three-line estimate: greens at a unit rate, ambers at a unit
rate with an error bar, reds listed individually as exclusions with the reason.
Utilities accept the exclusions because they can see which points they are and
why. A single-number estimate invites a haggle; a three-line one invites a
decision about the reds, which is the conversation worth having.

The discipline that makes it work is not lifting lids on visit one. Every time I
have broken that rule the dry visit has turned into a full survey and the method
has collapsed back into surveying everything for nothing.

I hold this at a flat $2,200 scoping fee, invoiced on delivery of the three-line
estimate, and it has never been the thing a client argued about.
